Hex plane partition

Given a flat-top hexagon with integer heights at each of its six corners, find the minimum number of planar regions that tile it.

    Algorithm

    A partition of the hex is induced by a set of non-crossing chords between its corners. The hex has C(6,2) − 6 = 9 chords (every corner pair minus the six adjacent pairs, which are hex edges). Two chords (a,b) and (c,d) cross iff their endpoints alternate around the perimeter — i.e. exactly one of c,d sits strictly between a and b.

    The solver:

    1. Enumerate every non-crossing chord subset by backtracking. For a hex this is ≈ 50 sets.
    2. For each set, repeatedly split the hex along chords to compute the resulting regions.
    3. Reject any region whose 3D corners aren't coplanar.
    4. Return the surviving partition with the fewest regions, breaking ties first by minimum total within-region height range, then by maximum total flat (horizontal) top-view area.

    Triangles are auto-planar, so any triangulation (4 triangles for a hex) is a valid fallback — the answer always lies in {1, 2, 3, 4}. Restricting to corner-vertex partitions is provably optimal: introducing Steiner points (interior or edge-midpoint vertices) can only subdivide regions further, never merge separately-coplanar ones.

    The tiebreaker matters because minimal partitions are not always unique. (0,1,2,3,2,1), for example, admits three distinct 3-region partitions: an asymmetric mirror pair (each total within-region range 7) and one mirror-symmetric partition (total range 3). Total range is invariant under any symmetry of the input, so mirror-symmetric heights naturally select a mirror-symmetric partition rather than landing on an arbitrary mirror image. Flat regions contribute zero to the range sum, so partitions with more strictly-flat regions still win when they exist ((0,0,0,1,1,1) picks the two-flat-triangle partition). The flat-area fallback handles cases like (0,0,0,1,0,1) where two candidates tie on range sum but one contains a flat quadrilateral and the other only a flat triangle.

    Coplanarity for regions with four or more corners is checked by testing every 4-tuple for a degenerate tetrahedron (zero triple product), not by fitting a plane through the first three points — the latter breaks when those three are nearly collinear in the hex plane and the normal becomes numerically tiny.

    The non-trivial coplanar quads — like the parallelogram in the (0,0,0,1,1,1) example — come from the three pairs of parallel chords on a flat-top hex: {02,35}, {04,13}, and {15,24}. A quad along one of these pairs is coplanar whenever the two chords have matching height differences along their parallel axis. That's also why "spiral" (0,1,2,0,1,2) resolves to 3 planes despite the two visible triangles having varying heights — the central quad (1,3,4,5) happens to be coplanar even though no two adjacent corners share a height.
